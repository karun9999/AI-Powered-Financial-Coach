Six features,
one question.

Can I afford this. Why did that bill jump. What is still charging me. Will we make it to the 30th. An Indian household asks the same thing six different ways — so the app should answer in rupees and dates, not in advice.

The shape of it

Don’t ship six products in a trench coat

Subscriptions, family budgets, tax, health score, cash‑flow, coach. Six features becomes six tabs if you let it, and then nothing talks to anything else. The way out is noticing that they all resolve to the same underlying object: a month with a shape. Every feature either explains that shape, threatens it, or improves it.

Score and tax are not tabs

Both get consulted a few times a year. The health score lives as a four‑bar strip on Today that pushes to a full screen; tax surfaces as a Today card that grows louder from January to March, plus a Control Centre glance during filing season.

The coach never gets a chat log

Threaded chat invites scrolling back. Each question opens a sheet, answers with a verdict and a chart, offers one action, and closes. History lives under Ask, sorted by what you did about it — not by what you typed.

One nudge at a time

The engine will find nine things wrong. Today shows exactly one, chosen by rupees saved per unit of effort. The other eight wait their turn. An app that lists all nine gets read as noise and dismissed as a block.

Signature component

The Runway

One chart, reused everywhere, learned once. Thirty bars, one per day, each the projected closing balance. A hairline marks the floor the user told us to keep. Bars falling into the band below it turn red and the band is shaded — so the answer to “am I going to be fine” is a shape, not a sentence.

27 Jul1 Aug · salary7 Aug · EMI18 Aug · card25 Aug

It reads as a tide, not a line chart

The sawtooth is the point. Salary on the 1st, rent on the 5th, EMI on the 7th, card on the 18th — an Indian salaried month has a fixed rhythm, and a smoothed line hides exactly the cliffs you need to see.

The floor is the user’s number

Not a computed minimum. People have a figure they get uncomfortable below, and it is rarely zero. Ask for it once during setup; everything red in the app is defined against it.

Same component, four sizes

96 pt hero on Today, 62 pt inside cards, 44 pt in the Lock Screen widget, and a bare 14 pt sparkline in list rows. Identical encoding at every size, so it is learned once.

Dots mark why

A small dot above a bar marks the event that caused the drop, coloured by kind. Tapping a bar opens that day: what clears, what is left, and what it would take to lift it back above the line.

Tab · Today

The screen is a forecast, not a statement

The balance at the top is today’s. Everything under it is about days that have not happened yet. If the app opens on a pie chart of last month’s spending, it has already failed the person holding it.

Lead with what is spendable

“₹38,420” is not the useful number — rent and the card autopay are already spoken for. Safe to spend ₹9,180 is the number that changes behaviour, and it sits directly under the balance.

The nudge is specific and free

“Move your SIP from the 10th to the 3rd” beats “consider reducing discretionary spending.” It names an amount, a date and a mechanism — and it costs the user nothing, which is why it is the one nudge shown.

Upcoming debits carry their consequence

Every row in Clearing this week shows the balance it leaves behind, not just the amount. ₹20,000 means nothing; leaves ₹12,820 means something.

Health is a pulse, not a page

Four bars, no numbers, one word each. It answers “is anything moving?” at a glance and pushes to the full screen when the answer is yes.

Sheet · over any tab

“Can I afford this?” is a date question

The honest answer is almost never yes or no. It is not yet, and the useful part is when. So the verdict names a date, and the chart shows the damage rather than describing it.

The answer is the chart

Two runways in one frame — today’s, and the one you would have. The segmented control cross‑fades between them. Nobody needs a paragraph explaining that six red bars is worse than three.

Cost is denominated in goals, not rupees

₹24,900 is abstract. Emergency fund 2.4 → 2.1 months and Goa trip: still yes are not. Show the trade against things the user has already told us they care about.

“Buy anyway” stays available

A coach that hides the decline button is a nag. The secondary action is real, it writes the purchase into the forecast, and the app never mentions it again.

Reminders are the actual product

Remind me 2 Aug turns a refusal into a plan. It schedules a notification for the day the answer changes — which is the only thing the person actually wanted.

On‑device, and it says so

The small pill earns its space. People will type their salary and their debts into this box, and a visible processing boundary is what buys you the second question.

Push · from Flow

“Why did my bill increase?” needs arithmetic, not empathy

This is the question a language model most wants to answer badly — a paragraph of sympathetic generalities. The right answer is a decomposition that adds up, ending on the one line the user can act on.

The four bars sum to the increase

₹980 + ₹520 + ₹210 + ₹130 = ₹1,840, exactly the jump. An explanation that does not reconcile is a guess, and users catch it immediately.

Separate what they control from what they don’t

A fixed‑charge revision and a fuel adjustment are not the user’s fault, and saying so builds more trust than a lecture about the air conditioning. The two they can move get the emphasis.

The slab crossing is the insight

Indian tariffs are slabbed. Crossing 400 units repriced the whole tier, so 48 units of restraint is worth ₹520 — far more than those units cost. This is the kind of thing an app can know and a person cannot.

Compare to last July, not last June

Bills are seasonal. Leading with 79% is technically true and practically misleading, so the card corrects itself at the bottom and lands on the real figure, 29%. Say the smaller true number even when the bigger one is more dramatic.

Push · from Ask

“How can I save ₹5,000?” is a request for a checklist

Not an essay, not a budget rebuild. Five named moves with rupee values, ranked by how little they hurt, with a total ticking toward the goal as each one is committed.

Ranked by pain, not by amount

Cancelling two dormant subscriptions saves less than cutting the holiday fund, and it goes first because it costs nothing. Sorting these lists by rupees is what makes the feature feel hostile.

Every row is executable

Each move maps to something the app can perform or deep‑link to — revoke a mandate, set a category cap, file a regime declaration. A suggestion with no button is a lecture.

The last resort is visibly last

Cutting the shared Goa goal is listed, greyed, and flagged as needing Priya’s agreement. Showing it builds trust; ranking it fifth shows judgement.

End by reframing the question

The closing card says the quiet part: ₹5,000 fixes this month, moving the SIP date fixes the pattern. A coach that only answers what was asked is a calculator.

Tab · Commitments

A subscription is a small EMI

Filed next to the home loan and the SIPs, a ₹649 streaming charge stops feeling like nothing. This screen turns 27 invisible mandates into one number, one worst offender, and one tap to stop it.

Price dormancy per use, not per month

₹500 a visit and ₹325 an hour do the persuading that ₹1,499 and ₹649 never will. Where usage cannot be read, fall back to “not opened in 5 months” — still a use‑based frame.

Duplicate billing is the headline feature

Paying for Hotstar through both a Jio plan and a card is common and invisible. Detecting it is worth more to a user than any chart on this screen, so it gets a bordered card above everything else.

Renewal urgency is a countdown, not a date

18 days next to an annual, non‑refundable ₹4,230 renewal. “14 Aug” is information; “18 days” is a deadline.

Cancelling means revoking the mandate

In India the charge usually rides a UPI autopay mandate or a card standing instruction. Name which one, then deep‑link into PhonePe or the bank’s mandate screen. An app that says “visit the merchant’s website” has done nothing at all.

Tab · Household

Shared money needs a privacy screen before a budget screen

The hard problem in family finance is not arithmetic, it is consent. A spouse seeing every merchant is how the feature gets switched off. So sharing is per‑category, per‑person, and legible the moment you turn it on.

Share totals by default, merchants never

Three toggles, all defaulted to the private setting, with a plain sentence spelling out what the other person actually sees. Priya knows groceries ran ₹2,400 over; she does not know where you shopped.

Goals are stacked, not summed

The Goa bar shows two segments in two colours. Who contributed what is the socially loaded part of a shared goal, and burying it inside a total is how resentment accrues.

A view‑only role is a real role

Amma can see the household budget and cannot change it. Multi‑generational households are the norm here, and a two‑role model — member and viewer — covers almost all of them.

Settle up once, without nagging

One request, sent as a UPI collect. No recurring reminders, no badge that persists. The app should not become the third participant in an argument about ₹4,320.

Push · from Household

Goals track themselves until they can’t

A bank transfer can only ever prove that money moved, never why. So the tracker leans on the strongest signal it has — a repeating transfer to a known destination — asks once to confirm what that signal means, and gives up cleanly to a person when there is no signal at all.

Confirm the pattern once, trust it after

The same recurrence engine that finds a dormant subscription can find a standing transfer. The first match asks — is this your Goa saving? — every match after that is tagged without a prompt.

Manual entry exists for what the bank never saw

A Diwali gift handed over in cash leaves no transaction to detect. Logging it by hand is not a fallback for a lesser feature; it is the only honest way that ₹5,000 becomes part of the goal at all.

Every row says how it got there

“Auto‑detected” and “Logged manually” sit in the same type size as the date. A person auditing the total should never have to guess which parts were inferred and which were declared.

An override, not a fight with the tracker

When automatic and manual tracking still don’t match what the bank app shows, editing the total directly beats making the user reconcile nine transactions to find the discrepancy. It overrides the figure without deleting the history that produced it.

Push · from Today

The health score is not a speedometer

The dial is the reflex, and it is wrong: one needle hides which of four independent things moved, and it invites comparison against a number nobody can interpret. Show the composition, and show it over time.

Lead with the direction

Steady, and improving before the number. 68 out of 100 means nothing in isolation; up 4 since June, and up 16 in a year, means something to everybody.

Each bar carries its own target notch

A dark tick at the target turns an abstract fill into a distance. Debt‑to‑income overshoots its notch and is the only red bar — the diagnosis reads before any label does.

Translate ratios into the user’s money

“Savings rate 22%” sits above “you keep ₹31,240 of every ₹1,42,000”. Ratios are for scoring; rupees are for deciding.

One recommendation, fully modelled

Clearing the car loan is named with its balance, its rate, the monthly cash it frees, the resulting score, and the reason not to do it yet. Recommendations that omit their downside stop getting read.

Push · from Commitments

Tax is a seasonal app inside the app

Dormant from April to December, loud from January to March. Two things matter — which regime you should be on, and how much 80C headroom is going unused — and both are decisions with a deadline, not reports.

The regime toggle recomputes live

Old against new, side by side, with the delta stated as a saving. This is the single highest‑value calculation in an Indian personal finance app, and it belongs at the top rather than inside a report.

Say when the saving actually arrives

Switching now lowers monthly TDS; switching in March means waiting for a refund the following July. Timing changes what the user does, so the card says it.

Headroom against the cap, with the cap drawn

₹1,12,000 of ₹1,50,000, with the remaining ₹38,000 called out. Making the ceiling visible is the whole reason the bar exists — the cap is what confuses people about 80C.

Turn the feature off when it stops applying

Switch to the new regime and 80C stops mattering — so the app says so and goes quiet. Features that keep prompting after they have become irrelevant are the main reason finance apps feel noisy.

Collect documents all year, silently

Rent receipts, the 80D premium, the home loan certificate. Filing season is a retrieval problem, so the work happens in the background for eleven months and surfaces as a checklist in the twelfth.

A coach that requires you to launch it has already lost. Most of the value lives on the Lock Screen, in a notification three days early, and in a Siri answer given while you are standing in the shop.

App Intents

“Ask my coach if I can afford this”

An AppIntent with a currency parameter, so the afford check runs from Siri, Spotlight, the Action button, or a Shortcut — and answers out loud with the verdict and the date.

Control Centre

Safe to spend, one swipe

A single control showing today’s spendable figure. During January to March it flips to days left before the 80C deadline.

Interactive widget

Kill a subscription from the Home Screen

The dormant subscription of the week, with a live Cancel button. The most valuable action in the app should not need the app.

Focus filter

Quiet during work hours

Nudges respect a Focus filter and batch to one evening digest. Financial anxiety at 11am helps nobody.

Trust

The consent screen is a design surface, not a legal one

This app reads bank data through the RBI account aggregator framework, and it asks people to type their salary into a text box. Both of those need to be designed with the same care as the charts.

Account aggregator consent

Scope, duration, revocation — on one screen

Name the accounts, the exact data types, the end date, and a revoke control that works without contacting support. Consent renewal arrives as a normal notification, not a lockout.

What the model sees

Balances and patterns, never account numbers

State it in the sheet where people are typing, not buried in settings. Redaction should be demonstrable: a tap that shows exactly what left the device, if anything did.

Biometric gate

Face ID on the app, not on every tap

Gate at launch and at anything that moves money. Blur balances in the app switcher. Do not make people authenticate to look at a chart.

First run

One number before any connection

Ask for the floor — the balance they get uncomfortable below — before asking for bank access. It is the only input the whole app is calibrated against, and it earns the next question.

Cold start

Useful at one month of data

Prediction needs history the app does not have yet. Show the runway with a visible confidence band that narrows over the first three months, and say plainly what it cannot know yet.

When the model is unsure

Say so in the verdict, not in a footnote

“Probably fine, but your income varies” is a legitimate answer for a freelancer. Confidence belongs in the headline, because that is the part people act on.

Design system

Colour, and what each one is allowed to mean

The palette comes from cheque security printing — the pale anti‑copy tint, the ledger ink, the madder rose of a stamp pad. The brand colour is deliberately kept out of the data, so red and green never have to compete with it for meaning.

Typography

Charter for prose, SF Pro for everything countable

The app itself is SF Pro throughout — it is an iOS app and should look like one. This document sets its argument in Charter, Matthew Carter’s ledger serif, because a design case is read and an app is scanned.

Every figure is tabular. font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums on all currency, or the runway’s day labels jitter as the numbers change.

Numbers

Lakh grouping, always

₹1,42,000 — not ₹142,000. Getting this wrong reads as a foreign product on day one. Abbreviate above a lakh in tight spaces (₹1.42L, ₹2.7Cr), never in a figure someone might act on.

Negative balances take a minus and the shortfall colour, never brackets. Round projections to the hundred; never round a number that already happened.

Motion

Only where it carries meaning

Runway bars grow from the baseline once, on first appearance, over 340 ms with a spring. The before/after cross‑fade in the afford check is 320 ms because comparison needs to feel simultaneous.

No confetti. Saving money is not a level‑up, and celebrating a ₹99 cancellation makes the app feel like a game rather than a coach.

Haptics

Three, and no more

A soft impact when a runway bar is scrubbed past the floor line. A success notification when a mandate is actually revoked. A warning when a coach verdict comes back negative. Everything else is silent.

Accessibility

The runway has to work without eyes

VoiceOver reads it as a summary first — “thirty days, lowest Wednesday the 29th, ₹12,820, below your floor” — then per‑day on swipe. Audio Graphs give it a pitch curve.

Nothing is encoded by colour alone: low days sit inside a shaded band and carry a label. Dynamic Type to accessibility sizes, which means every card reflows to a single column rather than truncating a rupee figure.

Dark mode

True black, tinted surfaces

OLED black for the ground so the Lock Screen widget blends, with cards lifted to a green‑tinted charcoal rather than neutral grey. Red and green both get lightened for contrast on dark — the light‑mode pair fails on black.

If this gets built

Where the hard parts are

Charts

Swift Charts, with a custom mark

The runway is a BarMark with a RuleMark floor and a shaded RectangleMark band. Scrubbing wants chartOverlay plus a selection binding, not a gesture recogniser.

Prediction

Recurrence detection first

Most of the forecast is not machine learning — it is finding repeating debits by amount, merchant and interval. Get that right and the runway is close to correct before a model touches it.

The model’s job

Explanation and ranking, not arithmetic

Compute the waterfall and the verdict in Swift so they always reconcile. Use the model to phrase them, rank the nudges, and answer follow‑ups — never to produce the numbers.

Onboarding

TipKit, after the first prediction

Teach the floor line the first time a red bar appears, not in a carousel on day one. The component explains itself once there is something in it worth explaining.
